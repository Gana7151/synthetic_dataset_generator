Quantitative Architectures for High-Fidelity Synthetic Tax Microsimulation: Integrating Deep Generative Models and Legislative Logic under the OBBBA Framework

The evolution of tax policy analysis has reached a critical juncture where traditional anonymization techniques for administrative data are increasingly vulnerable to re-identification through advanced computational attacks.1 As researchers and policymakers navigate the complexities of the U.S. federal tax code, the demand for high-fidelity synthetic datasets that preserve both the statistical properties and the logical integrity of the original records has never been higher.1 The challenge is compounded by the transition from the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) to the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), which introduces a suite of new above-the-line deductions and significant modifications to the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) structure.4 To meet these demands, quantitative models—ranging from deep generative adversarial networks to copula-based multivariate coupling—offer a transformative pathway to improve the accuracy, privacy, and utility of synthetic tax populations.3

Theoretical Frameworks for Synthetic Data Generation in the Tax Domain

The fundamental objective of synthetic data generation in the tax sector is to create a representative population of taxpayers whose individual records are entirely artificial, yet whose aggregate behavior and multivariate relationships mirror those found in confidential Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Statistics of Income (SOI) files.8 This transition from "partially synthetic" to "fully synthetic" data is motivated by the fact that even masked or blurred datasets can leak information when combined with external data sources.2 A fully synthetic public use file (PUF) is conceptualized as a random sample drawn from a statistical process that approximates the confidential administrative data generation process.2

Deep Generative Architectures: CTGAN and TVAE

Traditional synthetic reconstruction methods often relied on iterative proportional fitting (IPF) or simple parametric models, which frequently failed to capture the non-linear, multi-modal dependencies inherent in financial data.11 Modern quantitative improvements are centered on deep learning architectures designed specifically for tabular data, such as the Conditional Tabular Generative Adversarial Network (CTGAN).6 CTGAN addresses two primary hurdles in tabular synthesis: the presence of mixed data types (continuous income vs. discrete filing status) and the highly skewed, long-tailed distributions typical of high-net-worth individuals.13

By employing a "conditional generator" and "mode-specific normalization," CTGAN allows for the effective sampling of rare categories, such as Married Filing Separately (MFS) status or specific high-bracket income ranges, which are often "washed out" in standard statistical samplers.6 This is critical for tax revenue modeling, where the top 1% of earners contribute a disproportionate share of total revenue.4 The architecture uses a conditional vector to represent discrete features, ensuring that the generator can be trained to produce samples that follow the imbalanced distributions found in real-world tax filings.13

Complementary to GANs, Tabular Variational Autoencoders (TVAE) utilize a probabilistic encoder-decoder structure to learn a latent representation of the data.6 While TVAE is often praised for its training stability, it may occasionally produce smoother distributions that underestimate the extreme tail events—such as concentrated capital gains—that are vital for estimating the revenue impact of rate changes in the 37% bracket.17 The choice between CTGAN and TVAE often hinges on the specific analytical goals, with GANs generally preferred for high-fidelity realism in the tails and VAEs for overall distributional stability.17

Copula Theory and Multivariate Dependency Modeling

The core of any tax dataset is the complex web of dependencies between variables like wages, capital gains, age, and itemized deductions.8 Copula theory provides a mathematically rigorous framework for modeling these dependencies by decoupling the learning of marginal distributions from the joint probability structure.7 According to Sklar’s Theorem, any multivariate distribution can be represented by its marginals and a copula function that "couples" them together.20

In a tax context, where income components are rarely independent, the Gaussian copula has emerged as a powerful baseline model.7 It assumes that the underlying dependency structure can be mapped to a multivariate normal distribution, even if the marginals are highly skewed.7 For more complex scenarios involving non-linear or fat-tailed relationships—common in the transition between middle-income earners and ultra-high-net-worth personas—empirical copulas offer a non-parametric alternative that makes fewer assumptions about the functional form of the data.19

Legislative Transition: Modeling OBBBA Parameters

The introduction of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) necessitates a wholesale update of the quantitative parameters used in synthetic tax engines.4 The legislation introduces novel "above-the-line" deductions, significantly Alters the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) landscape, and modifies state conformity requirements.4

Above-the-Line Deductions and AGI Integrity

One of the most frequent errors in early synthetic tax specifications (v1 through v3) was the misclassification of OBBBA deductions as "below-the-line" itemized expenses.4 Corrective engineering (v4.0) identifies these as adjustments to gross income that flow through Schedule 1 Part II to Form 1040 Line 10.4 This distinction is mathematically significant because above-the-line deductions reduce Adjusted Gross Income (AGI), which in turn influences the phase-out of various credits and the calculation of state tax liabilities in non-conforming jurisdictions.4

Quantitative models must account for the specific eligibility criteria, such as the VIN position-1 constraint for car loan interest—which requires vehicles to be U.S.-assembled (VIN starting with '1', '4', or '5')—and the FLSA premium portion logic for overtime deductions.4 A failure to enforce these logic-based constraints leads to "synthetic hallucinations" where a persona might claim a deduction for a foreign-made vehicle, violating the explicit intent of the OBBBA.4

The Reversion of the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)

The OBBBA effectively ends the "taxpayer-friendly" AMT era of the TCJA.5 While the exemption amounts for 2026 are largely indexed for inflation, the income levels at which these exemptions begin to phase out have been drastically lowered to 2018 levels.26 Furthermore, the phase-out rate has doubled from 25 cents per dollar to 50 cents per dollar.4

For a single filer in 2026, the phase-out now starts at $500,000.4 A persona with an AMT income of $600,000, who would have faced no phase-out in 2025, now sees their $90,100 exemption reduced by $50,000 ($100k excess  0.50 rate), leaving only $40,100 exempt.4 This quantitative shift dramatically expands AMT exposure for earners in the $500,000 to $680,000 range.4 Synthetic datasets must capture this expansion to avoid systematically understating the tax liability of high-income personas.4

Quantitative Validation: KS Tests and Mahalanobis Distances

To improve the "work" of synthetic data generation, researchers must implement a multi-layered validation framework that measures fidelity, utility, and privacy.27 Statistical fidelity refers to the degree to which the synthetic data mimics the marginal and joint distributions of the original dataset.27

Microsimulation Integration: Tax-Calculator and TAXSIM

The ultimate test of synthetic tax data is its utility in downstream policy analysis.18 A synthetic dataset that passes all statistical tests but fails to accurately predict the revenue impact of a 1% rate hike is of limited value.28 To improve this "work," generators should integrate with established microsimulation models like the Policy Simulation Library (PSL) Tax-Calculator.44

The Role of Tax-Calculator in Automated Validation

The Tax-Calculator is an open-source model designed for federal individual income and payroll tax analysis.46 It permits the simulation of thousands of tax reforms by parameterizing policy variables (over 200 parameters).46 A robust synthetic pipeline can use the Tax-Calculator to perform record-by-record validation: the tax liability generated for a synthetic persona during the synthesis phase must match the liability calculated by the Tax-Calculator when the same record is treated as "new" data.46

This process mirrors the cross-model validation effort between the PSL Tax-Calculator and NBER's TAXSIM-35 model.48 By running 100,000 randomly generated units through both systems and ensuring the results differ by no more than one cent, researchers establish a rigorous baseline for what constitutes a "valid" tax record.48 Synthetic datasets can leverage this by using the Tax-Calculator's CLI tool (tc) or Python API to certify that their generated records are legally and mathematically sound.47

Data Preparation and "Aging" for Future Years

Synthetic populations often need to represent future tax years where administrative data does not yet exist.8 The Tax Policy Center (TPC) uses a "Look-Alike Public Use File" (LAPUF) process to "age" their data.49 This involves inflating dollar amounts by forecasted per capita growth rates (e.g., from the Congressional Budget Office) and using a constrained optimization algorithm to re-weight records to match future macro targets for return counts and AGI classes.25

Constraint-Augmented Generation (CAG) for Tabular Finance

While deep learning models are proficient at learning distributions, they are famously poor at learning exact linear constraints—such as .51 To improve synthetic data, quantitative researchers must employ Constraint-Augmented Generation (CAG).51

Enforcing Accounting Identities

The Synthetic Data Vault (SDV) library allows for the explicit declaration of business rules through its "Constraints" API.51 These rules ensure that synthetic data meets deterministic requirements 100% of the time.51 In the tax domain, this is essential for maintaining the integrity of the 1040 form hierarchy.

Inequality Constraints: Enforcing that Itemized_Deductions > Standard_Deduction for any record flagged as an "itemizer".51

Fixed Combinations: Ensuring that State = 'TX' never occurs with a non-zero State_Income_Tax.4

Logical Coherence: Using constraint programming to prevent "employed infants" or "retired children" from being synthesized, which preserves the semantic validity of demographic variables.29

Integrating Hard Linear Constraints into GANs

Advanced frameworks like the Sobolev Wasserstein GAN (SWGAN) use the Inequality Constraint Augmented Lagrangian Method to build constraints directly into the adversarial training objective.56 By penalizing the generator when it produces records that violate accounting identities, the model learns the "feasible region" of the tax code.56 This prevents "mode collapse" where the model might otherwise ignore rare but legally required combinations of attributes.52

Multi-State Jurisdictional Nuance and Non-Conformity

The quantitative improvement of synthetic tax datasets must also address the "multi-state problem"—the reality that state tax codes frequently diverge from federal law, particularly during major legislative shifts like the OBBBA.4

California: The Addition-Back Requirement

California (CA) generally does not conform to the new above-the-line OBBBA deductions.4 For a synthetic dataset to be "CA-valid," the generator must calculate a CA-specific AGI that adds back any federal deductions taken for tips, overtime, car loans, or senior status.4 Furthermore, CA has unique income tables for its Earned Income Tax Credit (CalEITC), which maximizes around $30,931 of earned income for the 2025 tax year.4 A quantitative model that simply replicates the federal EITC logic for CA personas would fail a basic accuracy audit.4

New York: The Itemization Threshold Shift

In New York (NY), the OBBBA increase of the SALT cap to $40,000 (up from $10,000) significantly changes the "itemization flip" threshold.4 Because NY requires taxpayers to itemize at the state level if they itemize federally, the synthetic generator must detect when a persona crosses this new federal threshold and automatically generate a corresponding NY Schedule A.4 For high-income personas (Level 3), the model must also generate IT-2105 estimated tax vouchers, which are required in NY if projected tax exceeds withholding by more than $300.4

Illinois: Longitudinal EITC Fluctuations

Illinois (IL) presents a specific longitudinal challenge for synthetic datasets spanning multiple years (2020–2025).4 The IL EITC is calculated as a percentage of the federal credit, but this percentage has increased over time: 18% in 2020, 20% from 2021–2023, 30% in 2024, and 40% in 2025.4 A quantitative engine that applies a flat 40% rate across all years would introduce significant error into longitudinal studies of poverty impact.4

Privacy-Preserving Frameworks and Differential Privacy

As researchers seek to release synthetic datasets to the broader public, the integration of Differential Privacy (DP) becomes a critical quantitative improvement.1 DP provides a mathematical guarantee that the risk of identifying any individual in the dataset is bounded by a privacy parameter, .59

DPCopula: Maintaining Utility under Privacy Constraints

The DPCopula method addresses the challenge of synthesizing high-dimensional tabular data with DP guarantees.7 It estimates the parameters of a copula function (such as the Gaussian correlation matrix) using differentially private Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) or Kendall’s .7 By adding noise to the dependency structure rather than to each individual point, DPCopula maintains better utility for complex multivariate queries than simple cell-level perturbation.7

The Validation Server Model

The Urban Institute’s "validation server" provides a secure environment for testing synthetic utility.1 This infrastructure allows external users to:

Develop: Build models and analytical pipelines on synthetic datasets that mirror the structure and statistical properties of administrative files.1

Submit: Upload their code to an automated prototype server.1

Execute: Run the code on real administrative tax data without ever seeing the sensitive records.1

Review: Receive results that have been vetted for disclosure risk, ensuring that no individual data is leaked in the aggregate output.1

This architecture effectively resolves the "utility-privacy" trade-off by allowing researchers to get "real" results while only ever touching "synthetic" data.1

Technical Implementation Pipeline for Synthetic Tax Data

To improve the existing work, the implementation of a synthetic tax engine should follow a modular, phase-based approach that prioritizes mathematical consistency and legislative compliance.4

Phase 1: The Tax Parameter Foundation (tax_tables.py)

The first and most critical step is the creation of a centralized repository for tax parameters.4 This module must be year-specific, accounting for inflation adjustments, bracket shifts, and legislative effective dates.4 For the OBBBA era, it must include phase-out tables for tip income, overtime, car loans, and senior deductions, as well as the reverted 2026 AMT start thresholds and doubled rates.4

Phase 2: Profile Generation and Semantic Mapping (profile_generator.py)

Profiles should be generated using probabilistic models (like Copulas) to ensure realistic pairings of age, filing status, and income sources.52 This module must include Boolean flags for OBBBA eligibility—such as is_tipped_worker or overtime_eligible—to drive the subsequent calculation logic.4 A specialized vin_generator.py should be used to ensure that car-loan personas have valid, U.S.-assembled VINs that pass cryptographic mod-11 check-digit tests.4

Phase 3: The Calculation Engine (federal_calculator.py)

The calculation logic must follow a rigorous sequence to maintain AGI integrity.4

Income Aggregation: Summing all 1040 income sources (W-2, 1099-NEC, Schedule B/D/E).4

Adjustments: Applying Schedule 1 adjustments, specifically the OBBBA deductions from Schedule 1-A, to arrive at AGI.4

Deductions: Calculating the maximum of standard or itemized deductions, applying the OBBBA-compliant SALT cap ($40,000 for 2025; $40,400 for 2026).4

QBI and Taxable Income: Applying the QBI deduction with year-specific phase-in ranges ($150,000 MFJ width for 2026).4

Shadow Tax Checks: Computing the AMT with the reverted 50-cent phase-out rate and the 2/37 rule for high-earning itemizers.4

Phase 4: Validation and Quality Assurance (validation.py)

Every generated dataset must be passed through a 15-rule validation engine.4 If a record fails any rule—such as W-2 Box 3 exceeding the Social Security wage base or Schedule C gross receipts not matching the sum of 1099-NEC inputs—it must be discarded and re-seeded.4 This ensures that the final 2,000-dataset corpus is free of the "math errors" that plagued previous non-quant versions.4

Conclusions

Quantitative models provide the necessary sophistication to transform synthetic tax data from a mere curiosity into a professional-grade tool for policy analysis.1 By integrating deep generative architectures like CTGAN for distributional fidelity, Copula theory for multivariate coupling, and Constraint-Augmented Generation for accounting integrity, researchers can significantly improve the accuracy and privacy of their work.6

The transition to the OBBBA legislative framework serves as a critical test of these quantitative systems.4 Accurate modeling of the reverted AMT phase-outs, the new 2/37 benefit cap, and the complex AGI-driven interactions between federal and state tax bases is essential for maintaining the analytical utility of synthetic datasets in the 2025–2028 window.4 Ultimately, the use of automated validation engines—incorporating metrics like Mahalanobis distance and KS tests, and benchmarking against industry standards like the PSL Tax-Calculator—ensures that synthetic tax populations are representative of the true filing population and legally compliant across all 50 states.4 This methodological rigor not only protects individual privacy but also democratizes access to high-quality tax data, enabling more transparent and data-driven public policy.1
